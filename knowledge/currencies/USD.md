# US dollar (USD)

## Central bank: the Federal Reserve
- **Mandate.** The Federal Reserve Act sets a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices (plus moderate long-term interest rates). The FOMC defines price stability as **2% inflation measured by the PCE price index**, not CPI. (Fed, goals of monetary policy)
- **Instrument.** The target range for the federal funds rate. Changes pass through to loan rates, asset prices, **exchange rates** and spending. (Fed, goals of monetary policy)
- **Meetings.** Eight scheduled FOMC meetings a year. Four of them (March, June, September, December) come with the **Summary of Economic Projections**, the "dot plot". For the rest of 2026, the meetings are **27–28 October** and **8–9 December** (with projections). Dates are tentative until confirmed at the meeting before. (Fed, FOMC calendar)
- **Reading the Fed.** The statement, the dot plot, the minutes (published three weeks after each meeting) and speeches by FOMC participants. Because the mandate is dual, a labour-market miss can offset a hot inflation print in the Fed's reaction function, and the reverse.

## Economy
- **Energy.** The US has been a **net total energy exporter since 2019** and a **net natural gas exporter since 2017**. It still imports crude oil on net, but exports refined products. (EIA)
  - *Implication (background):* unlike the euro area or Japan, an oil or gas price spike does not worsen US terms of trade on net. That is one reason energy shocks have tended to favour the dollar against energy importers.
- **Reserve currency.** *Background:* the dollar is the main reserve and funding currency of the world, so global stress raises demand for dollars (a haven bid), even when the stress starts in the US.

## Channels that move the dollar (background)
1. **Rate differentials.** The gap between US yields and those abroad, at the 2-year point for policy expectations and the 10-year for the global anchor. A Fed that tightens more than others widens the gap in the dollar's favour.
2. **Data surprises.** Payrolls, CPI/PCE and ISM move expectations for the Fed path.
3. **Risk sentiment.** In risk-off episodes the dollar usually rises with the yen and the franc. In calm, carry-seeking markets, higher-yielding currencies can outperform it.
4. **Fiscal and political risk.** Deficits, debt-ceiling episodes and tariff policy can add a risk premium, which works against the dollar even when yields rise.

## How to read USD in a pair
USD is the quote currency of EURUSD, GBPUSD, AUDUSD and NZDUSD, so **US strength is bearish for those pairs**. In USDJPY, USDCAD and USDCHF it is the base, so US strength is bullish. DXY is roughly 58% euro by weight (*background*), which is why it moves largely as an inverse of EURUSD.
